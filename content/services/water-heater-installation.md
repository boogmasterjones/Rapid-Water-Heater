        <h3>What a Water Heater Installation Actually Involves</h3>
        <p>A straightforward tank replacement is usually a few hours of work, but it is more than lifting one unit out and setting another in its place. We shut off the water and the gas or electrical supply, drain the old tank, and disconnect it before hauling it away. Setting the new unit involves fresh supply connections, a new temperature and pressure relief valve with its discharge line routed correctly, and a check of the drain pan and its drain line. On a gas unit we verify the venting draws properly rather than assuming the old configuration still meets code. Then the tank fills, the lines get purged of air, and we test the temperature at a fixture before we leave.</p>
        <p>Two things commonly surprise homeowners. The first is that a replacement usually requires a permit and an inspection, which is true across most of Sarasota, Manatee, and Charlotte County — a legitimate contractor pulls it rather than skipping it. The second is that older installations often were not up to current code, so bringing the new unit into compliance can mean adding a pan, correcting a discharge line, or installing an expansion tank that was not there before. We tell you about those items during the quote rather than after the work has started.</p>
        <h3>What Affects the Cost of a Water Heater Replacement</h3>
        <p>The unit itself is only part of the number. Capacity and fuel type set the baseline, and a heat pump or tankless system costs more up front than a standard tank. Beyond the equipment, the biggest variables are access and code. A tank in an open garage is a much simpler job than one in a tight interior closet or an attic platform, where the old unit has to come out through the same tight path the new one goes in. Any code corrections needed, plus permit fees and disposal of the old unit, make up most of the rest.</p>
        <p>Because those variables are specific to your home, we quote after looking at the actual installation rather than giving a number over the phone that changes once we arrive. That consultation is free, and the price we give you is the price you pay.</p>
        <h3>Choosing the Right Size for Your Household</h3>
        <p>Capacity in gallons is the number most people know, but first-hour rating is the more useful one. It describes how much hot water a unit can actually deliver during a busy hour, accounting for both what is stored and how quickly it reheats. Two tanks of the same gallon capacity can have meaningfully different first-hour ratings depending on the burner or element.</p>
        <p>As a general guide, a 40 to 50 gallon tank serves many two to three person households, and larger families or homes with multiple bathrooms running at once tend to need more capacity or faster recovery. What matters more than any rule of thumb is your actual pattern of use — several showers back to back in the morning puts a different demand on a system than the same amount of hot water spread across a day. We ask about that before recommending a size, because an undersized unit is the most common reason a brand new water heater still disappoints.</p>
