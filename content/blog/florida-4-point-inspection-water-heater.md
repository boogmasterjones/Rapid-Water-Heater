If you own an older home in Southwest Florida, sooner or later a 4-point inspection comes up. Your carrier asks for one when you apply for a policy, or when renewal time rolls around, and suddenly a stranger is in your garage with a flashlight and a clipboard.

Most homeowners we talk to aren't worried about the roof or the breaker panel. They're worried about the water heater in the corner that nobody has looked at since they moved in. Here's what the inspection actually covers, what gets written down about your water heater, and when replacing it ahead of time is the simpler path.

## What a 4-Point Inspection Covers

A 4-point inspection is a short report on four systems:

- **Roof** — covering, age, visible damage
- **Electrical** — panel, wiring type, visible hazards
- **Plumbing** — supply lines, drains, water heater, signs of leaks
- **HVAC** — age and condition of the heating and cooling equipment

It isn't a full home inspection. Nobody is grading your paint or your landscaping. The inspector documents the condition of those four systems and sends the report to whoever asked for it.

That last part matters. **The inspector doesn't make decisions about your insurance.** They record what they see. Your carrier reads the report and decides what it wants to do, and every carrier handles that differently.

## What the Inspector Looks At on Your Water Heater

The water heater sits in the plumbing section. Expect the report to note:

### Age

Almost always the first thing recorded. The inspector reads the **data plate**, the label on the side of the unit, which lists the manufacturer, model and serial number. On many brands the manufacture date is coded into the serial number, and each brand uses its own format.

Worth doing before the appointment: take a clear photo of that label with your phone. You'll want it for your agent anyway. Our post on [water heater age and Florida home insurance](/blog/water-heater-age-florida-home-insurance) walks through how to read it.

### Corrosion and Rust

Rust streaks on the tank, corrosion at the fittings, or crusty white or green buildup around the connections all get noted. In a Florida garage, with heat and humidity year-round, that kind of wear shows up on an aging tank.

### Leaks and Moisture

Any active drip gets written down, and so do the quieter signs: a stained floor under the unit, a rusty drain pan, dampness around the base, or mineral trails down the side of the tank.

### The T&P Valve and Discharge Line

The temperature and pressure relief valve is a safety device. The inspector checks that it's there and that it has a proper discharge line running where it's supposed to. A missing or improperly routed discharge line is a common note.

### The Installation Itself

Flexible connectors, the drain pan and its drain line, strapping or support where required, combustion air and venting on a gas unit, and the electrical connection on an electric one. Work done to code looks like work done to code.

## Why the Water Heater Is a Common Sticking Point

Two reasons, and they stack.

The first is that a tank water heater has a finite life. It sits full of hot water, year after year, and eventually the tank gives up. An aging unit can look fine on the outside and still be close to the end.

The second is that a leaking water heater is a water damage claim waiting to happen, and water damage is exactly what carriers are looking at when they ask for this report. So an older tank showing corrosion is the kind of note that draws attention to the plumbing section.

What any individual carrier does with that note is genuinely not something we can tell you. There's no universal age cutoff. Guidelines vary from carrier to carrier and they change over time, so **the only reliable answer comes from your own insurance agent.** Ask them directly what your policy requires and whether a 4-point report is coming.

## When Replacing It First Is the Simpler Path

Sometimes it makes sense to deal with the water heater before the inspection rather than after. Consider it if:

- **The unit is already past the age you'd be comfortable with.** If you look at the data plate and wince, you have your answer.
- **There's visible corrosion or any moisture at the base.** Those signs don't reverse themselves.
- **You're already seeing symptoms.** Rusty hot water, rumbling, or running out of hot water sooner than you used to. Our list of [7 signs it's time to replace your water heater](/blog/7-signs-its-time-to-replace-your-water-heater) covers what to watch for.
- **You're buying the home.** Check the data plate during your inspection period, before you close and before you shop for a policy.
- **You're coming up on renewal with an older unit.** Your agent can tell you what's expected and when.

The practical argument for going first is time, not drama. Replacing a water heater on your own schedule means you can compare tank, tankless and heat pump options, get a clear quote, and pick an install day that suits you. Replacing one against a deadline, or after it's already dumped forty gallons across the garage floor, means taking whatever is available that afternoon.

## Choosing the Replacement

If you're replacing anyway, it's a reasonable moment to think about what actually fits your home:

- **Tank (gas or electric).** The familiar option, and a straightforward swap in most homes.
- **Tankless.** Heats water as you use it, with no storage tank sitting there full. Good if you want the space back or plan to stay put a while. See our [tankless water heater installation page](/services/tankless-water-heater-installation).
- **Heat pump (hybrid).** Electric, and it pulls heat from the surrounding air. It needs room and airflow to work properly, so the location matters.
