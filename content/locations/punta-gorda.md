        <h3>Punta Gorda's Water Comes From Shell Creek</h3>
        <p>Punta Gorda is unusual among the cities we serve in that it runs its own water system rather than buying from a regional supplier. The city draws from Shell Creek, impounded behind the Hendrickson Dam into a reservoir of roughly 800 acres, and treats it at a plant that has been operating in some form since the 1960s. It is a surface water source fed by both Shell Creek and Prairie Creek.</p>
        <p>What that means for your water heater is straightforward. Surface water is treated before it reaches you, but it still carries dissolved mineral, and in a tank water heater that mineral drops out of suspension and settles at the bottom as sediment. Over years it forms a layer the burner or element has to heat through, which lengthens recovery times and shortens the life of the unit. This is the single strongest argument for an annual flush in Punta Gorda homes, and it is the first thing we look at when someone tells us their hot water does not last like it used to.</p>
        <h3>Two Storms and a Rebuilt Housing Stock</h3>
        <p>Punta Gorda's building stock tells the story of two hurricanes. Charley came through in August 2004 and destroyed or damaged a very large share of the city, which produced a wave of rebuilding through the following years. Ian followed in September 2022. The result is a city where a home's age tells you a great deal about what is behind the wall — and where three quite different situations sit side by side.</p>
        <p>Homes rebuilt after Charley were built to a considerably stricter code than what came before, and they are now reaching the age where the original water heater is due for replacement. Homes in the historic district that survived may still have decades-old supply lines that deserve a look while the heater is being swapped. And homes repaired after Ian vary widely depending on how thorough that work was. We have worked across all three and do not assume which one we are walking into.</p>
