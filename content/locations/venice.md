        <h3>Venice Runs Its Own Reverse Osmosis Plant</h3>
        <p>Venice is genuinely different from the rest of our service area when it comes to water. The city owns and operates its own reverse osmosis treatment plant near the Intracoastal Waterway, drawing brackish groundwater from wells in the intermediate aquifer rather than taking treated surface water from a regional supplier. That water is pushed through membranes that strip out dissolved minerals before it ever reaches your tap.</p>
        <p>For a water heater, that is good news. Reverse osmosis removes much of the mineral content that would otherwise settle into the bottom of a tank as sediment, so Venice homes on city water generally see less scale accumulation than homes a few miles away on a surface water system. It does not eliminate maintenance — an annual flush is still worthwhile, and the anode rod still does its job protecting the tank lining — but the sediment problem that shortens water heater life elsewhere in the region is typically milder here.</p>
        <h3>Island Homes, Seasonal Homes, and Storm History</h3>
        <p>The age divide between Venice Island and the newer mainland neighborhoods shapes most of the installations we do. On the island, tight utility closets and decades-old supply lines mean we plan the route for getting the old tank out before we bring the new one in. On the mainland and toward Wellen Park, access is easier and the work is more predictable.</p>
        <p>Venice also has a high proportion of seasonal residents, and that changes what good advice looks like. If a home sits empty for months, the most valuable thing an owner can do is shut off the water at the main before leaving, which eliminates almost all risk of returning to a flooded house from a failed supply line or a tank that let go. Setting the water heater to its vacation setting rather than leaving it heating an empty house is worth doing at the same time. We are happy to show homeowners where those controls are during any visit.</p>
